What is Q-Day?

The day quantum computers break today’s public-key encryption, and why organizations are preparing before it arrives.

Q-Day is the term for the moment a quantum computer becomes powerful enough to break the public-key cryptography that protects most digital systems today, including RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. Security professionals call such a machine a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC).

What happens on Q-Day?

A CRQC running Shor’s algorithm could derive private keys from public keys. That would let an attacker decrypt recorded TLS and VPN sessions, forge digital signatures, impersonate websites with fake certificates, sign malicious software updates and break into systems protected by public-key authentication. Symmetric encryption such as AES-256 would remain secure, but the keys that protect it are usually exchanged with public-key cryptography, so the damage would spread.

Q-Day is unlikely to be announced. The first organization to build a CRQC, possibly a nation state, may have strong reasons to keep it secret. That is one reason security agencies urge organizations to migrate before there is public proof that a CRQC exists.

When will Q-Day arrive?

No one knows. Estimates have moved closer as research advances. Quantum hardware is improving in qubit counts and error rates, and algorithm research keeps lowering the resources needed: a 2025 estimate by Google researcher Craig Gidney suggested RSA-2048 could be broken by a machine with fewer than one million noisy qubits in under a week, around twenty times fewer qubits than his 2019 estimate. Surveys of quantum experts by the Global Risk Institute show a meaningful probability of a CRQC within the next ten to fifteen years.

For planning, the exact date matters less than the gap between it and how long your data must stay secret and your migration will take. Our harvest now, decrypt later guide explains how to calculate that.

Q-Day vs Y2K

Q-Day is often compared to Y2K: a technology problem that touches almost every system and must be fixed in advance. There are two big differences. Y2K had a fixed date, while Q-Day does not. And data stolen before Q-Day can still be exposed after it, so the harm can begin before the day itself.

How to prepare for Q-Day

  • Build a cryptographic inventory so you know which systems depend on RSA and ECC.
  • Protect long-lived data first by enabling hybrid post-quantum key exchange.
  • Follow a structured PQC migration roadmap aligned to the NIST PQC standards.
  • Ask vendors for their quantum-safe roadmaps and add post-quantum requirements to contracts.
  • Brief your board: Q-Day is a business continuity and regulatory risk, not only a technical one.
