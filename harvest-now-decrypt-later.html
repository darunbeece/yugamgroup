Harvest now, decrypt later

Why data encrypted today is already exposed to tomorrow’s quantum computers, and what to do about it.

Harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL), also called store now, decrypt later, is an attack in which an adversary copies encrypted data today and keeps it until a quantum computer can break the encryption. It turns a future capability into a present-day risk.

How a harvest now, decrypt later attack works

An attacker does not need to break anything today. They only need to capture traffic or steal encrypted files: by tapping network links, compromising a cloud storage bucket, or exfiltrating backups. Most internet traffic uses RSA or elliptic-curve key exchange to agree the symmetric keys that protect a session. If that key exchange is recorded, a future quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm can recover the session key and read everything that followed.

Well-resourced nation-state groups are widely assumed to be collecting data this way already. National cyber agencies, including CISA, the NSA and the UK NCSC, cite harvest now, decrypt later as a main reason to start post-quantum migration before quantum computers exist.

Which data is at risk?

The question to ask is how long your data must stay confidential. If it only matters for a few days, the risk is low. If it must stay secret for ten years or more, it is exposed today. High-risk examples include:

  • health records, genetic data and insurance files
  • banking, trading and payment data, and customer identity information
  • intellectual property, product designs and source code
  • legal, merger and board communications
  • government, defence and critical infrastructure data

Mosca’s theorem: are you already late?

Researcher Michele Mosca put the risk into a simple inequality. Let x be how long your data must stay secret, y how long your migration to post-quantum encryption will take, and z how long until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. If x + y > z, data you protect today will be exposed. With migrations that take years and data that lives for a decade, many organizations are already inside that window. How to defend against harvest now, decrypt later

  1. Switch key exchange first. Hybrid key exchange that combines X25519 with ML-KEM protects recorded TLS and VPN traffic even if one algorithm fails. This is the single most effective step.
  2. Find long-lived data. Use a cryptographic inventory to see which systems carry data with a long confidentiality life.
  3. Reduce what can be harvested. Limit data retention, segment networks and tighten access to backups and archives.
  4. Use strong symmetric encryption at rest. AES-256 remains quantum-resistant; make sure the keys that wrap it are protected by sound HSM key management.
  5. Plan signatures next. Signatures cannot be harvested in the same way, but certificates and firmware signing need long lead times, so schedule the move to ML-DSA early.
