Crypto agility and cryptographic inventory

Know where your cryptography lives, then make the next algorithm change a configuration change.

Crypto agility, sometimes written cryptoagility, is the ability to replace cryptographic algorithms, keys and libraries quickly and safely without redesigning the systems that use them. A cryptographic inventory is the map that makes it possible. Together they are the foundation of every post-quantum migration.

Why crypto agility matters now

Past algorithm changes, from DES to AES and from SHA-1 to SHA-2, took most organizations a decade or more, largely because cryptography was hard-coded deep inside applications and devices. The move to post-quantum cryptography is bigger: it touches key exchange, signatures, certificates and hardware all at once. New attacks on algorithms can also appear without warning, as several post-quantum candidates showed during the NIST process. An agile design lets you respond in weeks instead of years.

What a cryptographic inventory covers

A cryptographic inventory records where and how cryptography is used across your estate. A useful one includes:

  • algorithms, key lengths and modes in use, such as RSA-2048, ECDSA P-256 or AES-GCM
  • certificates, their issuers, lifetimes and where they are deployed
  • protocols and configurations: TLS versions and cipher suites, SSH, IPsec and VPNs
  • cryptographic libraries and versions in source code and dependencies
  • keys held in HSMs, cloud KMS and secrets managers, and who owns them
  • cryptography inside vendor products, SaaS services and embedded devices
  • the data each system protects and how long it must stay confidential

Many teams record this as a cryptographic bill of materials (CBOM), a machine-readable format supported by the CycloneDX standard, so it can be generated in pipelines and kept current rather than becoming a one-off spreadsheet.

How to build a cryptographic inventory

  1. Combine discovery methods. Network scanning finds TLS endpoints and certificates; source code and binary analysis finds libraries and hard-coded algorithms; HSM, KMS and certificate authority exports find keys.
  2. Ask vendors. Much enterprise cryptography sits inside third-party products. Ask suppliers which algorithms they use and when they will support ML-KEM and ML-DSA.
  3. Attach business context. Link each finding to an owner, a system and a data classification so you can prioritize.
  4. Automate it. Generate the inventory in CI/CD and from infrastructure as code so it stays accurate.

From inventory to cryptographic posture management

Once an inventory exists, cryptographic posture management keeps it useful: continuous monitoring that flags weak or deprecated algorithms, expiring certificates, unmanaged keys and policy violations as they appear, and tracks progress toward quantum-safe targets. It turns a one-time discovery exercise into an ongoing control that auditors and boards can rely on.

Engineering for crypto agility

  • Centralize cryptography behind shared libraries or services instead of calling algorithms directly in application code.
  • Make algorithms and parameters configuration, governed by policy-as-code, not constants.
  • Design protocols and data formats with algorithm identifiers and room for larger keys and signatures.
  • Keep certificate lifetimes short and issuance automated so new algorithms roll out with normal renewals.
  • Test new algorithms in staging continuously, with a clear rollback path.
