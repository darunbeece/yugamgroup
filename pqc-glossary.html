Post-quantum cryptography glossary

Plain-English definitions of the terms you will meet in a PQC program.

Post-quantum security comes with a lot of new vocabulary. This glossary explains the terms most often used in quantum-safe migration projects, standards and vendor material.

Asymmetric (public-key) cryptography

Cryptography that uses a public key and a private key, such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. It is used for key exchange and digital signatures, and it is the part a quantum computer threatens.

CBOM (cryptographic bill of materials)

A machine-readable list of the cryptographic assets in a system: algorithms, keys, certificates, libraries and protocols. It is the output of a cryptographic inventory.

CNSA 2.0

The NSA’s Commercial National Security Algorithm Suite 2.0, which sets quantum-resistant algorithms and deadlines for national security systems. See CNSA 2.0 explained.

Composite certificate

A certificate whose key and signature combine a classical and a post-quantum algorithm, valid only if both verify. See post-quantum PKI.

CRQC (cryptographically relevant quantum computer)

A quantum computer large and reliable enough to break RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography in a practical time.

Crypto agility

The ability to change cryptographic algorithms and keys quickly without redesigning systems.

Cryptographic posture management

Continuous monitoring of an organization’s cryptography, including weak algorithms, expiring certificates and policy violations, built on top of an up-to-date inventory.

FIPS 203, 204 and 205

The NIST standards, published in August 2024, that define ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA. See NIST PQC standards.

Grover’s algorithm

A quantum algorithm that speeds up brute-force search. It roughly halves the effective strength of symmetric keys, which is why AES-256 is recommended.

Harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL)

Collecting encrypted data today to decrypt it once quantum computers can. See harvest now, decrypt later.

HSM (hardware security module)

Tamper-resistant hardware that generates and protects cryptographic keys. See HSM key management.

Hybrid key exchange

Combining a classical algorithm, such as X25519, with a post-quantum one, such as ML-KEM, so a connection stays secure if either holds. It is the usual first step in TLS and VPN migration.

KEM (key encapsulation mechanism)

A method for two parties to agree a shared secret key using public-key cryptography. ML-KEM is a KEM.

Lattice-based cryptography

Cryptography built on hard problems involving high-dimensional lattices. ML-KEM, ML-DSA and FN-DSA are lattice-based.

ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA

The first three NIST post-quantum algorithms: ML-KEM for key establishment, ML-DSA for signatures and SLH-DSA as a hash-based signature backup.

Mosca’s theorem

The rule that if the time your data must stay secret plus your migration time exceeds the time until a CRQC exists, you are already at risk.

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC)

Classical algorithms designed to resist attacks by both classical and quantum computers. See post-quantum cryptography explained.

Q-Day

The day a CRQC can break today’s public-key cryptography. See what is Q-Day?

QKD (quantum key distribution)

Exchanging keys using quantum physics over dedicated optical links. See QKD vs PQC.

Quantum-safe

Also quantum-resistant or quantum-proof: describes cryptography, products or systems that are expected to withstand attacks from quantum computers.

Shor’s algorithm

The 1994 quantum algorithm that can factor large numbers and compute discrete logarithms efficiently, breaking RSA, Diffie-Hellman and ECC.

Stateful hash-based signatures (LMS, XMSS)

Quantum-safe signature schemes that must track which one-time keys have been used. They are approved for firmware signing in NIST SP 800-208.
