Quantum key distribution vs PQC

Two very different routes to quantum-safe encryption, and how to choose between them.

Quantum key distribution (QKD) and post-quantum cryptography (PQC) both aim to keep data safe from quantum computers, but they work in completely different ways. QKD uses quantum physics and special hardware; PQC uses new mathematics in ordinary software. Understanding the difference is the first step in any quantum cyber security strategy.

How quantum key distribution works

QKD sends encryption keys encoded in the quantum states of individual photons over fiber or free-space links. The laws of quantum mechanics mean that anyone who measures those photons disturbs them, so the two parties can detect eavesdropping. Once a key is shared, it is used with conventional symmetric encryption such as AES. BB84, published in 1984, is the best-known QKD protocol.

Limits of QKD

  • Special hardware and links. QKD needs dedicated optical equipment and, typically, dedicated fiber. It cannot run over the ordinary internet.
  • Distance. Photon loss limits direct links to roughly a few hundred kilometers, so longer networks rely on trusted relay nodes, each of which becomes a target.
  • No authentication on its own. QKD still needs a way to authenticate the two ends, which usually means classical or post-quantum signatures.
  • Implementation risk. Real devices have been attacked through side channels that the theory does not cover.

For these reasons the NSA does not recommend QKD for national security systems, and the UK NCSC and several European agencies advise organizations to prioritize post-quantum cryptography.

Why most enterprises choose post-quantum cryptography

Post-quantum cryptography runs on existing computers, networks, browsers and cloud services. It protects key exchange and digital signatures, scales to millions of connections, and is standardized by NIST. It can be rolled out as software and firmware updates rather than new infrastructure. That makes PQC the practical choice for quantum-safe encryption across an enterprise, with QKD reserved for specialized, high-value point-to-point links.

Quantum random number generators

A quantum random number generator (QRNG) uses quantum effects to produce unpredictable random numbers, which are the raw material for strong keys. QRNGs can strengthen key generation, but they do not protect against quantum attacks on RSA or ECC by themselves. Validated random number generators inside HSMs and operating systems already meet the requirements for most organizations.

QKD vs PQC at a glance

  • Basis: QKD uses physics; PQC uses mathematics.
  • Infrastructure: QKD needs dedicated hardware and links; PQC runs on existing systems.
  • Scope: QKD only distributes keys; PQC covers key exchange and signatures.
  • Standards: PQC has NIST FIPS 203, 204 and 205; QKD standards are still maturing.
  • Best fit: PQC for enterprise-wide migration; QKD for niche, short-range, high-security links.
