NIST PQC standards

FIPS 203, 204 and 205, what comes next, and the dates that NIST post-quantum cryptography guidance sets for enterprises.

The NIST post-quantum cryptography standards are the algorithms the US government, and most of the industry, will use to replace RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. This guide explains what each NIST PQC standard does and how it affects your systems.

How NIST chose the post-quantum algorithms

In 2016 the National Institute of Standards and Technology opened a public competition for quantum-resistant public-key algorithms. Cryptographers around the world submitted 82 candidates, and over several rounds the community attacked them in public. Some well-known candidates were broken along the way, which is exactly what the process is for. In July 2022 NIST announced its first selections, and in August 2024 it published them as final Federal Information Processing Standards.

FIPS 203: ML-KEM

The Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber, is the primary NIST PQC standard for key establishment. It replaces Diffie-Hellman and RSA key transport in protocols such as TLS, SSH and IPsec VPNs. It comes in three parameter sets, ML-KEM-512, ML-KEM-768 and ML-KEM-1024. ML-KEM-768 is the common default, and hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768 key exchange is already enabled in major browsers and content delivery networks.

FIPS 204: ML-DSA

The Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium, is the main post-quantum signature for certificates, code signing and document signing. Signatures and public keys are a few kilobytes, much larger than ECDSA, so certificate chains, firmware images and constrained devices need testing.

FIPS 205: SLH-DSA

The Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, derived from SPHINCS+, relies only on the security of hash functions. It is slower and produces larger signatures than ML-DSA, but its conservative design makes it a good fit for long-lived roots of trust and firmware signing.

What comes next: FN-DSA and HQC

NIST is finalizing FN-DSA, based on Falcon, as a further signature standard with smaller signatures. In March 2025 it also selected HQC, a code-based key-encapsulation mechanism, as a backup to ML-KEM so that not all post-quantum key establishment rests on lattices. Stateful hash-based signatures (LMS and XMSS, in NIST SP 800-208) are already approved for specific uses such as firmware signing.

NIST post-quantum timelines

NIST’s draft transition report, IR 8547, proposes that quantum-vulnerable algorithms offering 112-bit security, such as RSA-2048 and ECC P-256, are deprecated after 2030 and that all quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms are disallowed after 2035. US National Security Memorandum 10 sets 2035 as the goal for federal systems, and the NSA’s CNSA 2.0 suite asks national security system vendors to move earlier, with some categories expected to be quantum-safe by 2030. Regulators in financial services and healthcare are following the same direction.

What the NIST PQC standards mean for your organization

  • Expect vendors to ship ML-KEM and ML-DSA support in libraries, HSMs, load balancers and cloud services over the next few years, and ask for their roadmaps now.
  • Plan for hybrid deployments first, then pure post-quantum once interoperability is proven.
  • Test performance and message sizes, especially for TLS handshakes, certificate chains and IoT devices.
  • Use FIPS 140-3 validated implementations where compliance requires them.
  • Start with a cryptographic inventory so you know which systems the standards apply to.
